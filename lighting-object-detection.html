Does an object detector still work once the sun goes down?

A YOLOv8n object detector trained and compared across day and night driving scenes, testing whether pretrained weights and optimizer choice matter more than expected once lighting gets hard.

Team · 6 members Dataset · nuScenes mini + custom Bandung footage Model · YOLOv8n

Overview

A camera only sees what the light lets it see

Self-driving perception leans heavily on cameras, and cameras are picky about light. This project trained an object detector to spot cars, pedestrians and motorcycles from a front-facing camera, then deliberately tested it on the conditions cameras struggle with most: dim morning light and full night.

Four training variants were compared side by side: pretrained versus trained-from-scratch weights, each combined with two different optimizers (SGD and AdamW), to see which combination held up best once the easy daytime frames ran out.

4model variants: pretrained/scratch × SGD/AdamW
404training images, nuScenes mini front camera
3classes tracked: car, pedestrian, motorcycle

How it works

Starting from what a model already knows, or starting from zero

YOLOv8n was chosen for being lightweight enough to train on limited compute while staying accurate enough to be a fair test. Two of the four models started from COCO-pretrained weights and fine-tuned onto the driving dataset (transfer learning); the other two started from a random initialization and had to learn every visual feature, from edges to car shapes, from scratch.

Technical detail: data and training setup

Training data came from nuScenes mini 1.0, a real-world autonomous-driving dataset, using only the front camera feed. The original 3D bounding boxes were projected into the 2D image plane and converted to YOLO's normalized (x-center, y-center, width, height) label format, discarding boxes smaller than 3 pixels as noise.

  • Pretrained models loaded COCO-trained weights and fine-tuned onto the day/night driving classes.
  • Non-pretrained models loaded only the YOLOv8n architecture definition, with all weights randomly initialized.

Learning rate (0.01 vs 0.001), image size (640 to 960px) and optimizer (SGD vs AdamW) were swept to find each model's best configuration, evaluated on mAP@0.5:0.95 and inference speed.

Results on nuScenes

On the benchmark itself, the difference was already visible

Before testing anywhere new, all four models were first scored on nuScenes' own day and night front-camera images, the same distribution they were trained on, shown above. Model 3 (pretrained, AdamW) reached mAP@0.5 of 0.79 on day scenes and 0.81 at night, at roughly 6 to 7 milliseconds per image, the strongest balance of accuracy and speed of the four. Model 4 (non-pretrained, AdamW) was the weakest at night, dropping to an mAP@0.5 of 0.55.

A counterintuitive result

Within nuScenes itself, both pretrained models scored slightly higher at night than during the day (Model 3 went from 0.79 to 0.81 mAP@0.5). Both non-pretrained models did the opposite, dropping at night, most sharply Model 4, from 0.75 down to 0.55. Pretrained features seemed to carry across lighting conditions; features learned from scratch did not.

Testing beyond the dataset

A brand new location separated the models even further

nuScenes performance is still performance on nuScenes. The harder test was new photos and video collected around a semi-structured Bandung street, a location and camera the models had never seen, captured at both morning and night. The pretrained AdamW model (Model 3) again held onto correct, confident boxes on cars and pedestrians in both conditions, while the from-scratch models lost detections as the light dropped.

What actually mattered

Starting from pretrained weights mattered far more than the optimizer choice, on nuScenes and even more so on the unfamiliar Bandung footage. Model 3 was reliable enough on both to become the one tested in real-time video inference.

Conclusion

404 images is a small dataset, and it shows

Every model could detect cars, motorcycles and pedestrians to some degree in both lighting conditions, but none did so perfectly. Two limits stood out: the training set was small at only 404 images, which caps how much any of the four variants could generalize, and RGB cameras are fundamentally light-hungry, so low-light frames simply carry less usable signal regardless of how the model was trained.
